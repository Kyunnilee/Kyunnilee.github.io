### Start of Agentic Robotics?

My research has always played with Vision-Language Models (VLMs), and as I begin my PhD, I've been excited to see how much progress they have made. When I first met them, they were mostly used as visual assistants: understanding images, doing VQA tasks, or generating captions.

Now, I see the advance of VLMs, where they can reason about physical environments, plan actions, and even execute them through robotics control. I've been seeing some exciting demos on Twitter that shows this progress. This excites me showing a new direction in robotics, where VLMs can serve as both high level planners and low-level executors.

But despite this excitement, there hasnt been studies yet that diagnostically studies on how useful VLMs are in controlling robots. "Can these models really reason about the physical world? Can they predict what will happen, recognize failures, and adapt their actions based on feedback?"

### Agentic Physical Reasoning

In closed loop settings, VLMs choose and adjust actions based on feedback. Physical reasoning plays an important role here, where physical properties of the scene often determines the outcome of actions.

Look at the two videos below, where the two robots are given the same task "Use a hammer to drive a nail". Both initially grasp the hammer in a way that causes it to tilt, but the outcome differs on how they refine their answers. The success case shows that the model observes the instability and adjusts its grasp closer to the hammer head. The failure case on the other hand continues with the unstable grasp and eventually fails. We can obviously get the fact that understanding the physical world requires not only observation, but also actively acquiring physical information and using it to guide our actions.

## Can additional physical information improve VLMs' physical reasoning and actions? 
<!-- Where does the failure arise in agents? Is it due to lack of information? or is it because of reasoning? -->

When vision-language models (VLMs) fail at physical reasoning tasks, an important question is whether these failures arise from missing physical information or from the models' inability to reason with that information. For example, if a model incorrectly predicts how an object will move, is it because the model does not know the object's mass or friction coefficient, or because it cannot correctly use these values to predict the outcome?

To investigate this question, we conduct an oracle analysis, where we provide models with ground-truth physical parameters obtained directly from simulators. By comparing model performance with and without this additional information, we aim to understand whether providing accurate physical parameters can overcome existing reasoning failures.


This question is particularly important for real-world embodied systems, where robots often receive additional physical information through sensors during interactions.

<div class="cards">
  <div class="card-a">
    <h4><i class="fa-solid fa-hand"></i>A hand with tactile sensors</h4>
    Consider a robot using a Sharpa hand equipped with tactile sensors to manipulate an object. As the robot touches and grasps the object, its sensors continuously provide information about contact, force, pressure, and the object's softness or stiffness. These signals help the robot understand the physical properties of the object and determine whether its current action is appropriate. If the object begins to slip, the robot may need to adjust its grip force. If the object is soft or fragile, the robot may need to reduce the applied force to avoid damaging it.
  </div>
  <div class="card-a">
    <h4><i class="fa-solid fa-car"></i>A car following another car</h4>
    When a car approaches another vehicle, its sensors provide information such as the distance to the front car, the speed of both vehicles, and their relative velocity. These physical values are critical for deciding whether the car should accelerate, slow down, or maintain its speed. As the environment changes, the vehicle continuously receives updated information and adjusts its actions accordingly.
  </div>
</div>

Both examples illustrate an important aspect of physical intelligence: successful actions depend not only on understanding visual observations, but also on interpreting physical information and using it to make better decisions.

<div class="qbox">
  This motivates two related questions:
  <ol>
    <li>Can VLMs effectively use additional physical information to improve their reasoning and predictions?</li>
    <li>Can VLMs use updated physical information to refine their decisions and improve actions during closed-loop interactions?</li>
  </ol>
</div>

### Experimental design: oracle analysis and physical parameter ablations

To investigate these questions, we conduct controlled experiments in which we provide VLMs with ground-truth physical parameters from simulators, such as object mass, friction, contact forces, material properties, and motion states.

Rather than providing all physical information at once, we group simulator parameters by physical property and introduce them incrementally. This allows us to examine how model performance changes as more physical information becomes available.

For example, when predicting how far an object will slide after being pushed, a model may initially receive only an image and a high-level task description. We then provide additional information about the surface material, followed by its friction coefficient, the object's mass, or the applied force. At each stage, we evaluate whether the additional information improves the model's prediction.

We further conduct ablation studies to identify which groups of physical parameters contribute most to performance improvements. In Regimes 1 and 3, we apply this incremental parameter-release approach to evaluate how different types of physical context affect model predictions and decisions. For closed-loop tasks, we also examine whether updated physical information helps models correct previous decisions and improve subsequent actions.

The oracle analysis allows us to distinguish between two possible sources of failure:

<div class="cards">
  <div class="card-a">
    <h4><i class="fa-solid fa-eye-slash"></i>Missing physical information</h4>
    The model initially fails because important physical properties are not observable or cannot be reliably inferred from visual inputs. Providing ground-truth parameters improves its performance.
  </div>
  <div class="card-a">
    <h4><i class="fa-solid fa-brain"></i>Limited physical reasoning ability</h4>
    The model continues to fail even when accurate physical parameters are explicitly provided, suggesting that the limitation is not only missing information but also difficulty reasoning with it.
  </div>
</div>

Through these experiments, we aim to identify which physical properties are most important for different tasks, how effectively VLMs use explicit physical information, and whether additional sensory feedback can improve closed-loop decision-making.

Ultimately, our goal is to understand whether the limitations of current VLMs come from what they cannot observe or from what they cannot reason about, even when the necessary physical information is available.

### What we found

<span class="exp-num">Finding 1</span>
<p class="exp-title">More information helps prediction, but only the right information</p>

On cup capacity, the six-model mean goes from 37.7% to 69.7%, and almost all of the gain comes from one step: the cup's inner shape. On visible object state, the extra values don't help at all.

<span class="exp-num">Finding 2</span>
<p class="exp-title">In actions, the extra information helps some models and hurts others</p>

- **Push to zone:** Gemini 4 Argon goes from 58% to 88% success within ten trials once it knows the mass and friction.
- **Seesaw balancing:** Qwen3.5-9B drops from 64% to 44% with the extra values.
- **Tool use (high-level skills):** Argon goes from 8 to 9 tasks, while Opus drops from 8 to 7 and Sol from 6 to 4. Models mention the properties in their reasoning but rarely use them in their code.

(Stars in the "Success within <i>k</i> trials" chart in the Regime 3 section show these oracle runs.)

## Can models learn from previous trials? (Regime 2)

<span class="exp-num">Finding 3</span>
<p class="exp-title">The first two trials matter most</p>

Accuracy rises most after the first one or two observed trials. More trials don't always help, and open-weight models can get worse.

<span class="exp-num">Finding 4</span>
<p class="exp-title">Trying helps, but how you try matters</p>

On the seesaw, every model improves with more trials (Argon reaches 96%). On push, GPT-6 Sol reaches 100% while the others reach 58–70%. Argon pushes too gently: it restarts only 3 times, compared with 31–40 for the other models, and runs out of trials.

## Takeaways

1. **RQ1:** Models can infer some physical properties from images, but miss hidden ones.
2. **RQ2:** Past trials and true physical values help, mostly early and mostly when they are the decisive ones.
3. **RQ3:** Knowing the physics is not enough: execution is the main bottleneck for action.

<div class="note">
  <b>Limitations.</b> Simulation only; few tool tasks; no human baseline.
</div>
