## Start of Agentic Robotics?

My research has always played with Vision-Language Models (VLMs), and as I begin my PhD, I've been excited to see how much progress they have made. When I first met them, they were mostly used as visual assistants: understanding images, doing VQA tasks, or generating captions.

Now, I see the advance of VLMs, where they can reason about physical environments, plan actions, and even execute them through robotics control. I've been seeing some exciting demos on Twitter that shows this progress. This excites me showing a new direction in robotics, where VLMs can serve as both high level planners and low-level executors.

But despite this excitement, there hasnt been studies yet that diagnostically studies on how useful VLMs are in controlling robots. Can these models infer physical properties from what they see? Can they learn from what happened in earlier attempts? And can they act, watch the result, and fix their actions?

## Agentic Physical Reasoning

In closed loop settings, VLMs choose and adjust actions based on feedback. Physical reasoning plays an important role here, where physical properties of the scene often determines the outcome of actions.

Look at the two videos below, where the two robots are given the same task "Use a hammer to pull out a nail". Both initially grasp the hammer in a way that causes it to tilt, but the outcome differs on how they refine their answers. The success case shows that the model observes the instability and adjusts its grasp closer to the hammer head. The failure case on the other hand continues with the unstable grasp and eventually fails. We can obviously get the fact that understanding the physical world requires not only observation, but also actively acquiring physical information and using it to guide our actions.

A model can fail for two reasons. It may not see the property that matters. Or it may see it, but not reason with it. To tell these apart, we run an **oracle analysis**: we give the model the true values from the simulator, one group at a time, and check what changes.

<details class="setup">
  <summary>Why give models the true values?</summary>
  Real robots get such values from sensors. A hand with tactile sensors feels contact, force and slip, and loosens or tightens its grip. A car measures the distance and speed of the car ahead, and decides to brake or accelerate. If a VLM can't use these values when they are handed over, more sensors won't help it.
</details>

## Can models understand the physical world from observations?

<span class="regime-chip r1">R1 · Prediction</span>

<div class="finding">
  <span class="exp-num">Finding 1</span>
  <p class="exp-title">More information helps prediction, but only the right information</p>
  <ul>
    <li><b>Hidden properties are hard to infer from images.</b> From images alone, the six-model mean on cup capacity is 37.7%.</li>
    <li><b>Extra information can help a lot.</b> With the true values, it rises to 69.7%. Most of the gain (+24.7 points) comes from one step: the cup's inner shape.</li>
    <li><b>More information does not always help.</b> On visible object state, accuracy falls from 54.3% to 49.7% as values are added.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Can models actively discover hidden physics?

<span class="regime-chip r3">R3 · Active Experimentation</span>

<div class="finding">
  <span class="exp-num">Finding 4</span>
  <p class="exp-title">Trying helps, but how you try matters</p>
  <ul>
    <li><b>Models improve through repeated experiments.</b> On the seesaw, every model gets better with more trials. Argon reaches 96%.</li>
    <li><b>Exploration strategies differ a lot.</b> On push, Argon restarts after overshooting only 3 times. The other models restart 31–40 times.</li>
    <li><b>More attempts don't mean better use of feedback.</b> Argon pushes too gently and runs out of trials at 58%. GPT-6 Sol reaches 100%.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Can models recognize and recover from failures?

<span class="regime-chip r3">R3 · Failure Recovery</span>

<div class="finding">
  <span class="exp-num">Finding 6</span>
  <p class="exp-title">Seeing a failure is not the same as fixing it</p>
  <ul>
    <li><b>Models can tell that something went wrong.</b> They notice failures, such as a hammer tilting in the grasp.</li>
    <li><b>Few fixes work.</b> Only 15–26% of attempts that follow a failure make progress.</li>
    <li><b>Recognizing a failure does not guarantee a good fix.</b> In the opening video, Argon sees the hammer tilt and regrasps it. Sol turns the hammer without fixing the grasp, and drops it.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Takeaways

1. **Physical understanding:** Models miss hidden properties in images. The right value helps a lot; the wrong ones can mislead.
2. **From knowledge to action:** Knowing the physics helps strong models act better. It can hurt weaker ones, and it rarely reaches the code.
3. **Learning from interaction:** The first one or two trials help most. How a model experiments decides whether trying pays off.
4. **Embodied execution:** Execution is the bottleneck. Low-level control is hard, and seeing a failure rarely leads to a working fix.

<div class="note">
  <b>Limitations.</b> Simulation only; few tool tasks; no human baseline.
</div>
