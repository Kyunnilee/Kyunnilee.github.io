## Agentic Physical Reasoning

My research has always played with Vision-Language Models (VLMs), when I first met them, they were just a simple "chat bot" as visual assistants: I would use to test image captioning, VQA skills or simply test if they understand images. And now begining my PhD, I'm very excited to see how much progress they have been made, where we have to call them real agents.

VLMs can now reason about physical environments, plan actions, and even execute them through robotics control. I've been recently seeing very exciting demos on Twitter that shows this progress. This excites me showing a new direction in robotics, where **VLMs can serve as both high-level planners and low-level executors**.

But despite this excitement, I havent met studies yet that diagnostically studies on **how useful VLMs are in controlling robots**. So I wanted to ask this question starting my first project in Berkeley: <it>Can these models infer physical properties from what they see? Can they learn from what happened in earlier attempts? And can they act, watch the result, and fix their actions?</it>

In closed loop settings, VLMs choose and adjust actions based on feedback. In the physical world world, physical reasoning plays an important role here, where **physical properties of the scene often determines the outcome of actions**.

Look at the two videos below, where the two robots are given the same task "Use a hammer to pull out a nail". Both initially grasp the hammer in a way that causes it to tilt, but the outcome differs on how they refine their answers. The success case shows that the model **observes the instability** and **adjusts its grasp** closer to the hammer head. The failure case on the other hand continues with the unstable grasp and eventually fails. We can obviously get the fact that understanding the physical world requires not only observation, but also **actively acquiring physical information and using it to guide our actions**.

To study this, we carefully created (1) **three evaluation regimes** to test this,(2) generated data in simulation for a controlled setting (<span class="apro-name">APRO</span> dataset), and (3)conducted a **Oracle setting evaluation and analysis** (Oracle settings) to disentagle how useful VLMs are in controling robots as agents in the physical world.

## Measuring agentic phsyical reasoning under three evaluation regimes

<a href="{{ '/assets/img/blog/apro/fig1_regimes.svg' | relative_url }}" target="_blank" title="Open full size"><img class="regime-fig" src="{{ '/assets/img/blog/apro/fig1_regimes.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Three evaluation regimes of APRO"></a>

<div class="caption">Three Evaluation Regimes for <span class="apro-name">APRO</span>.</div>

The simplest way to understand physical perception to action, we question

- **R1**: Can the model understand and predict physics?
- **R2**: Can the model learn from previous observations?
- **R3**: Can the model actively interact with the physical world and accomplish a task?

The three regimes represent increasing levels of interaction and agency.

<details class="setup">
  <summary>In the real world we dont get parameter values?: We have sensors, we get to know why they fail</summary>
  Consider a robot using a Sharpa hand with tactile sensors to manipulate an object. As the robot touches and grasps the object, its sensors continuously provide information about contact, force, pressure. <b>Given these parameter signals</b> from the sensors, <b>can robots use this information well to manipulate correctly during action?</b>
  
  Here is one more example: In autonomous driving, when a car approaches another vehicle, its sensors provide information such as the distance to the front car, the speed of both vehicles, and their relative velocity. <b>These physical values are critical for deciding</b> whether the car should accelerate, slow down, or maintain its speed. As the environment changes, the vehicle continuously receives updated information and adjusts its actions accordingly.
</details>

To tell these apart, we run an **oracle analysis**: we give the model the true values from the simulator, one group at a time, and check what changes.

## What we learned

1. **Physical perception is a major bottleneck.** Revealing one key physical property can improve model accuracy by over 20 points. Revealing irrelevant information has little negative impact.
2. **Models differ in how well they maintain their correct answers.** New observations correct wrong answers at similar rates across models. However, they change correct answers to wrong ones in at most 11% of cases for proprietary models and 15–31% for open-weight models.
3. **Models reason with information well, but struggle to gather it through actions themselves.** VLMs can infer physics from good observations and can reason with physical values once they are given, but they struggle to choose actions that produce good observations.

### Models can use evidence to improve their prediction

We tested models on Regime 1 tasks on properties that can be estimated by observation. (i.e, Geometry, volume, height), models often misses the hidden properties that decides the answer. In oracle settings, (step by step information released in evidence groups), 6 of 7 models could improve accuracy (+21.4 points on average across the four R1 tasks, from images only to all evidence);

On cup capacity task, the six-model mean climbs from 37.7% to 69.7%, and **the largest jump comes when the most relevant cue appears**: the cup's inner shape.

### Predicting from experience? (R2) Does it align to action and adaptation performance? (R3)

We designed the same task setting (push box, balance the seesaw) as in R2 and R3, where in R2 settings, the model should predict an outcome **given** a series of trials. In R3 settings, **the model should act on the evironment**, retreive the physical property hints from their actions (each trial might reveal something), and **refine** their answers.

**Improvement in oracle settings**

What if the model is told the physical parameter values to utilize it durin interaction? We added a ablation to test how much models can improve or will they change actions when given this critical hints.

**Information utilization is where it distinguishes strong models**: Look at the push task results (the arrows at the right edge), Argon goes from 58% to 88% within ten trials, where without this information, they collapsed similarly with open-weight models, yet given this information, they could use it well in their reasoning that clearly distinguishes the performance of these 4 models. Below explains why Argon could not have higher performance (which is due to conservative action generation), while with this information, they could overcome their action direction to reach the goal more efficiently.

**Extra physical knowledge does not consistently help execution.** With the true properties, Argon goes from 8 to 9 tasks, while Opus drops from 8 to 7 and Sol from 6 to 4. The properties show up in the reasoning, but **rarely in the executed code**.

## Takeaways

1. **Physical understanding:** Models miss hidden properties in images. The right value helps a lot; the wrong ones can mislead.
2. **Learning from interaction:** Trials help, but how a model explores decides whether trying pays off.
3. **Embodied execution:** Execution is the bottleneck. Seeing a failure rarely leads to a working fix.

  <b>Limitations.</b> Simulation only; few tool tasks; no human baseline.
