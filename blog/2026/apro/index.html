Measuring Agentic Physical Reasoning

Created on November 23, 2026

2026   ·   robotics   vlm   benchmark   ·   research

Heekyung Lee, Zirui Wang, Fiona Ryan, David M. Chan, Trevor Darrell, Yutong Bai · UC Berkeley

TL;DR

We build APRO, a simulation benchmark of 480 items across 11 tasks that tests whether VLMs can use physical reasoning to act, from predicting physics, to learning from earlier trials, to controlling a robot in closed loop. We evaluate 7 VLMs and run an oracle analysis: we hand models the true physical values from the simulator to separate what they can't see from what they can't reason with.

Two key findings:

  • Physical information improves prediction. With the true values, cup-capacity accuracy rises from 37.7% to 69.7%.
  • Turning physical knowledge into successful actions is still hard. Models use feedback unevenly, and only 15–26% of attempts after a failure make progress.

Start of Agentic Robotics?

My research has always played with Vision-Language Models (VLMs), when I first met them, they were just a simple “chat bot” as visual assistants: I would use to test image captioning, VQA skills or simply test if they understand images. And now begining my PhD, I’m very excited to see how much progress they have been made, where we have to call them real agents.

VLMs can now reason about physical environments, plan actions, and even execute them through robotics control. I’ve been recently seeing very exciting demos on Twitter that shows this progress. This excites me showing a new direction in robotics, where VLMs can serve as both high-level planners and low-level executors.

But despite this excitement, I havent met studies yet that diagnostically studies on how useful VLMs are in controlling robots. So I wanted to ask this question starting my first project in Berkeley: Can these models infer physical properties from what they see? Can they learn from what happened in earlier attempts? And can they act, watch the result, and fix their actions?

Agentic Physical Reasoning

In closed loop settings, VLMs choose and adjust actions based on feedback. In the physical world world, physical reasoning plays an important role here, where physical properties of the scene often determines the outcome of actions.

Look at the two videos below, where the two robots are given the same task “Use a hammer to pull out a nail”. Both initially grasp the hammer in a way that causes it to tilt, but the outcome differs on how they refine their answers. The success case shows that the model observes the instability and adjusts its grasp closer to the hammer head. The failure case on the other hand continues with the unstable grasp and eventually fails. We can obviously get the fact that understanding the physical world requires not only observation, but also actively acquiring physical information and using it to guide our actions.

To study this, we carefully created (1) three evaluation regimes to test this,(2) generated data in simulation for a controlled setting (APRO dataset), and (3)conducted a Oracle setting evaluation and analysis (Oracle settings) to disentagle how useful VLMs are in controling robots as agents in the physical world.

Measuring agentic phsyical reasoning under three evaluation regimes

Three Evaluation Regimes for APRO.

The simplest way to understand physical perception to action, we question

  • R1: Can the model understand and predict physics?
  • R2: Can the model learn from previous observations?
  • R3: Can the model actively interact with the physical world and accomplish a task?

The three regimes represent increasing levels of interaction and agency.

APRO Dataset

We generated a dataset, APRO (Agentic Physical Reasoning Olympiad), simulator grounded, we organized the tasks under the three evaluation regimes mentioned above.

Examples of APRO dataset. APRO contains 480 evaluation items across 11 tasks.

In the real world we dont get parameter values?: We have sensors, we get to know why they fail Consider a robot using a Sharpa hand with tactile sensors to manipulate an object. As the robot touches and grasps the object, its sensors continuously provide information about contact, force, pressure. Given these parameter signals from the sensors, can robots use this information well to manipulate correctly during action? Here is one more example: In autonomous driving, when a car approaches another vehicle, its sensors provide information such as the distance to the front car, the speed of both vehicles, and their relative velocity. These physical values are critical for deciding whether the car should accelerate, slow down, or maintain its speed. As the environment changes, the vehicle continuously receives updated information and adjusts its actions accordingly.

To tell these apart, we run an oracle analysis: we give the model the true values from the simulator, one group at a time, and check what changes.

With APRO, we evaluated 7 models (4 properity models, and 3 open-weight models): Gemini 4 Argon, GPT-6 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash,Claude Opus 5.5, Qwen3.5-27B, Qwen3.5-9B, and DeepSeek-VL2 .

What we learned

  1. Physical information improves prediction. Given the true physical values, most models can utilize this information for a better prediction, and this leads to better action under a high level goal(R3).
  2. Feedback helps, but models use it unevenly. On push-to-zone, GPT-6 Sol solves every scene within 10 trials; the other models stop at 58–70%.
  3. Physical reasoning does not guarantee execution. Even when models notice a failure, only 15–26% of the next attempts make progress.

R1: Inferring physical properties

R1 · Prediction

Models can use evidence to improve their estimates. From images alone, models often miss the hidden properties that decide the answer. When we give them the true simulator values, the mean accuracy of six of the seven models rises from 49.2% to 63.1% (+13.9 points); DeepSeek-VL2 does not benefit.

We add the values one group at a time. On cup capacity, the six-model mean climbs from 37.7% to 69.7%, and the largest jump comes when the most relevant cue appears: the cup’s inner shape.

R2: Predicting from experience

R2 · Evidence-Conditioned Revision

Here the model sees k earlier trials of the same scene, then predicts the next outcome. The first one or two observations help every model the most. After that, more trials do not always mean better predictions: open-weight models can get worse as more trials are added. On the seesaw, Qwen3.5-9B falls from 56% with one trial to 40% with ten.

Models correct their first mistakes, but how fast depends on the task. On push, GPT-6 Sol, Gemini 4 Argon and Claude Opus 5.5 fix at least half of their first mistakes by the second observation and 87–95% in the end. On the seesaw, they fix only 50–69%, and need 3–6 observations to fix half.

R3: Adapting through active interactions

R3 · Active Interaction

R3 asks whether models can choose their own actions, watch what happens, and reach a goal. We test two settings: physical principle discovery (symbolic actions on the seesaw and push tasks) and tool manipulation (writing robot code).

Physical principle discovery

In seesaw balancing and push-to-zone, the model picks an action, sees the result, and tries again, for up to 10 trials. Each trial reveals something about mass or friction.

The benefit of interaction differs across tasks. On the seesaw, all models improve with more trials, and Gemini 4 Argon reaches 96%. On push, GPT-6 Sol reaches 100%, while the other models stop at 58–70%.

Why does Argon do well on the seesaw but not on push? It plays it safe. Argon restarts after overshooting the target only 3 times (others: 31–40) and keeps pushing gently, so it runs out of trials before reaching the zone. Using feedback is not enough; the model also has to take actions that make enough progress.

Improvement in oracle settings

What if the model is told the physics it would otherwise have to discover? In the oracle setting, we give it the true mass and friction up front. Strong models turn this into better actions: on push, Argon goes from 58% to 88% within ten trials, as the stated values overcome its cautious pushes. Qwen3.5-9B cannot use the information, and its seesaw success collapses from 64% to 44%.

Tool manipulation

R3 · Tool Use

Low-level control is a major bottleneck. With high-level skills, Argon and Opus finish 8 of 10 tool tasks; with raw joint control on the same Sharpa hand, they finish 6 and 4. GPT-6 Sol shows the largest gap (6/10 vs. 0/10): it can plan the task but not execute it. Qwen3.5-27B fails even with skills (1/10), often ending the task without checking the outcome.

Extra physical knowledge does not consistently help execution. With the true properties, Argon goes from 8 to 9 tasks, while Opus drops from 8 to 7 and Sol from 6 to 4. The properties show up in the reasoning, but rarely in the executed code.

Seeing a failure does not guarantee a better action. In the opening video, Argon sees the hammer tilt, regrasps it, and succeeds; Sol turns the hammer without fixing the grasp and drops it. Across episodes, only 15–26% of attempts after a failure make progress.

Takeaways

  1. Physical understanding: Models miss hidden properties in images. The right value helps a lot; the wrong ones can mislead.
  2. Learning from interaction: Trials help, but how a model explores decides whether trying pays off.
  3. Embodied execution: Execution is the bottleneck. Seeing a failure rarely leads to a working fix.
Limitations. Simulation only; few tool tasks; no human baseline.
