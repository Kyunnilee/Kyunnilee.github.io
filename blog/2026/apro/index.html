APRO: Measuring Agentic Physical Reasoning

Created on November 23, 2026

2026   ·   robotics   vlm   benchmark   ·   research

Heekyung Lee, Zirui Wang, Fiona Ryan, David M. Chan, Trevor Darrell, Yutong Bai · UC Berkeley

TL;DR

We find that

We build APRO, a simulator benchmark that tests whether VLMs can use physical reasoning to act, not just answer questions about physics.

Giving models the true physical values helps them predict, but turning that knowledge into successful robot actions is still hard.

Paper · Code · Dataset

Start of Agentic Robotics?

My research has always played with Vision-Language Models (VLMs), and as I begin my PhD, I’ve been excited to see how much progress they have made. When I first met them, they were mostly used as visual assistants: understanding images, doing VQA tasks, or generating captions.

Now, I see the advance of VLMs, where they can reason about physical environments, plan actions, and even execute them through robotics control. I’ve been seeing some exciting demos on Twitter that shows this progress. This excites me showing a new direction in robotics, where VLMs can serve as both high level planners and low-level executors.

But despite this excitement, there hasnt been studies yet that diagnostically studies on how useful VLMs are in controlling robots. Can these models infer physical properties from what they see? Can they learn from what happened in earlier attempts? And can they act, watch the result, and fix their actions?

Agentic Physical Reasoning

In closed loop settings, VLMs choose and adjust actions based on feedback. Physical reasoning plays an important role here, where physical properties of the scene often determines the outcome of actions.

Look at the two videos below, where the two robots are given the same task “Use a hammer to pull out a nail”. Both initially grasp the hammer in a way that causes it to tilt, but the outcome differs on how they refine their answers. The success case shows that the model observes the instability and adjusts its grasp closer to the hammer head. The failure case on the other hand continues with the unstable grasp and eventually fails. We can obviously get the fact that understanding the physical world requires not only observation, but also actively acquiring physical information and using it to guide our actions.

To study this, with my team, we carefully created three evaluation regimes to test this, generated data in simulation for a controlled setting, and conducted a fine-grained evaluation and analysis to disentagle how useful VLMs are in controling robots as agents in the physical world.

Attempts after a failure that actually make progress

A model can fail for two reasons. It may not see the property that matters. Or it may see it, but not reason with it. To tell these apart, we run an oracle analysis: we give the model the true values from the simulator, one group at a time, and check what changes.

Missing information

The property can't be seen or inferred from the image. Giving the true value fixes the answer.

Limited reasoning

The model still fails with the true value in hand. The problem is using the information, not getting it.
Why give models the true values? Real robots get such values from sensors. A hand with tactile sensors feels contact, force and slip, and loosens or tightens its grip. A car measures the distance and speed of the car ahead, and decides to brake or accelerate. If a VLM can't use these values when they are handed over, more sensors won't help it.

Part 1 · Physical Understanding

Can models understand the physical world from observations?

R1 · Prediction

Finding 1

More information helps prediction, but only the right information

  • Hidden properties are hard to infer from images. From images alone, the six-model mean on cup capacity is 37.7%.
  • Extra information can help a lot. With the true values, it rises to 69.7%. Most of the gain (+24.7 points) comes from one step: the cup's inner shape.
  • More information does not always help. On visible object state, accuracy falls from 54.3% to 49.7% as values are added.

Part 2 · From Knowledge to Action

Does knowing the physics translate into better actions?

R3 · Oracle Analysis

Finding 2

In actions, extra information helps some models and hurts others

  • True mass and friction can improve action success. On push-to-zone, Gemini 4 Argon goes from 58% to 88%.
  • Some models do worse, even with accurate values. On seesaw balancing, Qwen3.5-9B drops from 64% to 44%.
  • Models mention the properties, but don't use them. In tool use, the values show up in the reasoning but rarely in the code. Argon goes from 8 to 9 tasks; Opus drops from 8 to 7, and Sol from 6 to 4.

Part 3 · Learning from Interaction

Can models use feedback to improve their predictions?

R2 · Evidence-Conditioned Revision

Finding 3

The first two trials matter most

  • Previous trials improve predictions. One observed trial lifts every model above random.
  • Most of the gain comes early. The biggest jumps happen after the first one or two trials.
  • More evidence does not guarantee better predictions. On the seesaw, Qwen3.5-9B falls from 56% with one trial to 40% with ten.
Accuracy given k prior trials
Each prompt shows k earlier trials of the same scene, then asks for the next outcome.

Fixing mistakes is easier on some tasks than others. On push prediction, the strongest models fix 87–95% of their first mistakes. On the seesaw, they fix only 50–69%, and need 3–6 trials to fix half.

Can models actively discover hidden physics?

R3 · Active Experimentation

Finding 4

Trying helps, but how you try matters

  • Models improve through repeated experiments. On the seesaw, every model gets better with more trials. Argon reaches 96%.
  • Exploration strategies differ a lot. On push, Argon restarts after overshooting only 3 times. The other models restart 31–40 times.
  • More attempts don't mean better use of feedback. Argon pushes too gently and runs out of trials at 58%. GPT-6 Sol reaches 100%.

Part 4 · Embodied Execution

Can models translate physical reasoning into successful actions?

R3 · Tool Use

Finding 5

Low-level control is the bottleneck

  • High-level skills give better task completion. With skills, Argon and Opus finish 8 of 10 tool tasks.
  • Raw joint control is still hard. On the same hand with joint control, they finish 6 and 4. GPT-6 Sol drops from 6 to 0.
  • Planning a task and executing it are different abilities. Models that plan well with skills can't carry the plan out joint by joint.

Can models recognize and recover from failures?

R3 · Failure Recovery

Finding 6

Seeing a failure is not the same as fixing it

  • Models can tell that something went wrong. They notice failures, such as a hammer tilting in the grasp.
  • Few fixes work. Only 15–26% of attempts that follow a failure make progress.
  • Recognizing a failure does not guarantee a good fix. In the opening video, Argon sees the hammer tilt and regrasps it. Sol turns the hammer without fixing the grasp, and drops it.

Takeaways

  1. Physical understanding: Models miss hidden properties in images. The right value helps a lot; the wrong ones can mislead.
  2. From knowledge to action: Knowing the physics helps strong models act better. It can hurt weaker ones, and it rarely reaches the code.
  3. Learning from interaction: The first one or two trials help most. How a model experiments decides whether trying pays off.
  4. Embodied execution: Execution is the bottleneck. Low-level control is hard, and seeing a failure rarely leads to a working fix.
Limitations. Simulation only; few tool tasks; no human baseline.
